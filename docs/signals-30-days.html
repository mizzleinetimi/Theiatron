DAY 02

Phases, movements, and the ring-and-barrier diagram

A phase is a movement getting its green; a cycle is one full trip through all the phases. In North American practice the through movements are the even phases (2 and 6 on the main street, 4 and 8 on the side street), the left turns are the odd ones, and they are arranged in two rings separated by a barrier the rings must cross together so main-street and side-street movements never overlap. Left turns come in protected, permissive, and protected-permissive flavours (the flashing yellow arrow). Pedestrians get WALK, then a flashing DON'T WALK clearance.

Own these
  • phase vs cycle (never confuse)
  • NEMA phase numbering 1–8
  • ring 1 / ring 2 / barrier
  • concurrent phases (2+6, 4+8)
  • protected · permissive · protected-permissive
  • flashing yellow arrow (FYA)
  • WALK / flashing DON'T WALK (FDW)
  • ped clearance
Do

Draw the ring-and-barrier diagram for a standard eight-phase junction. Then draw Theiatron's: it is a two-phase controller (NS / EW, no protected lefts). Practise saying that out loud, unprompted, because an engineer will spot it in ten seconds and respect you for naming it first.

DAY 03

Timing parameters and the change interval

Cycle length, splits, and the intervals inside a phase: minimum green, maximum green, the yellow change interval (set by a kinematic formula from approach speed, typically 3–6 s) and the all-red clearance interval (crossing width over speed, typically 1–2 s). Between every phase you lose time to startup and clearance; that lost time, about 4 s per phase, is the single most important number in adaptive control, because every extra switch pays it again.

Own these
  • cycle length · split · offset
  • min green · max green
  • yellow change interval
  • all-red clearance interval
  • startup lost time · clearance lost time
  • effective green
  • dilemma zone
  • passage / extension time
Do

Compute yellow and all-red for a 50 km/h approach by hand. Then open configs/intersection.yaml: yellow 3 s, all-red 1 s, min green 12 s, max green 45 s. You already discovered in the parameter sweeps that each switch costs about 6 s of capacity — that is lost time, measured. Say it that way.

DAY 04

Pretimed, actuated, coordinated — the control modes

Pretimed (fixed-time) signals run the same plan regardless of traffic, often with time-of-day plans. Semi-actuated signals keep the main street green until a detector calls the side street. Fully actuated signals extend each green as vehicles keep arriving and end it when a gap appears (gap-out) or the maximum is reached (max-out). Coordinated-actuated signals do this inside a fixed background cycle so a corridor stays in step. The insight for your pitch: actuation responds to presence at the stop bar, not to queues or approaching platoons — the information gap a camera fills.

Own these
  • pretimed / fixed-time
  • time-of-day (TOD) plans
  • semi-actuated · fully actuated
  • call · detector actuation
  • gap-out vs max-out
  • recall (min, max, ped, soft)
  • coordinated-actuated
  • force-off · yield point

DAY 05

Adaptive signal control: forty years of prior art

Adaptive systems have existed since the 1980s. SCOOT (TRL, UK) and SCATS (Sydney) are centralised: they tune cycle, split and offset for whole networks from loop data. Surtrac (Pittsburgh, CMU spin-out), InSync and ACS-Lite are decentralised, intersection-by-intersection. Reported benefits: FHWA generally cites around 10% average delay improvement across deployments, vendor pilots claim 20–40%. The question an investor should ask, and you should answer first: if this has existed for decades, why isn't it everywhere? Cost, detection maintenance, communications, and scarce engineering staff. That is Theiatron's thesis in one sentence.

  • SCOOT · SCATS · UTOPIA
  • Surtrac · InSync · ACS-Lite · SynchroGreen
  • ASCT (FHWA's acronym)
  • detection dependency
  • degree of saturation (SCATS' core measure)

DAY 06

Detection: the Achilles heel

Inductive loops cut into the pavement (cheap-ish, die with resurfacing), video detection with virtual zones (Iteris, Autoscope — struggles at night, in rain, with occlusion: exactly the objections you'll hear), radar (Wavetronix, good for dilemma-zone protection), the GRIDSMART fisheye camera at the centre of the junction (closest form-factor analogue to your unit), thermal (FLIR), wireless magnetometers (Sensys), and probe data. Every actuated or adaptive system is only as good as its detection, and detection is what fails first in the field.

Week 2Measuring "better"

The performance vocabulary. This is the week that converts "44% less waiting" into claims an engineer can't wave away.

DAY 08

Flow, capacity, and saturation

Volume is vehicles per hour; capacity is what a lane can discharge on green. The base saturation flow rate is 1,900 passenger cars per hour per lane, about one vehicle every 2 s once a queue is moving. Capacity is saturation flow times the share of the cycle that is green (g/C). The v/c ratio says how close to the edge you are; above about 0.9 the queue never fully clears. Motorcycles, buses and trucks are converted with passenger-car equivalents, which matters enormously in okada- and keke-heavy Nigerian traffic.

Own these
  • volume · flow rate · peak-hour factor
  • saturation flow rate (1,900 pc/h/ln)
  • saturation headway (~2 s)
  • g/C ratio · capacity
  • v/c · degree of saturation
  • critical lane volume
  • PCU / PCE
Do

DAY 09

Control delay and Level of Service

The industry's headline metric is control delay per vehicle: the total extra time caused by the signal, including slowing, queue move-up, stopped time and accelerating away. It maps to Level of Service grades: A up to 10 s, B to 20, C to 35, D to 55, E to 80, F beyond. Reporting "we cut control delay from 10.9 s to 6.0 s, LOS B to LOS A" is the sentence funders and engineers are wired to hear. Also know stops, queue length (average and 95th-percentile back-of-queue) and throughput.

Own these
  • control delay (not just stopped delay)
  • uniform · incremental · initial-queue delay
  • LOS A–F thresholds
  • stops per vehicle
  • 95th-percentile queue
  • throughput
  • travel time index
Do

Restate Theiatron's results as LOS. Then explain why the 95th-percentile figure (36.7 s → 18 s) matters more than the average to a commuter and to a politician. Be ready to volunteer that the demo footage is light traffic and that the SUMO benchmark will cover peak conditions.

DAY 10

Webster's method — the classic you will be tested on

Webster (1958) gave the industry its formula for the delay-minimising pretimed cycle: C₀ = (1.5L + 5) / (1 − Y), with L the total lost time per cycle and Y the sum of the critical flow ratios. Greens are then split in proportion to each phase's flow ratio. Every adviser will ask whether your fixed-time baseline was even tuned. The answer is "the current baseline is a 30 s split; a Webster-optimised baseline and a NEMA actuated baseline are in the benchmark plan" — and you should be able to compute Webster's cycle on a napkin.

Own these
  • Webster's optimal cycle
  • critical flow ratio (v/s)
  • sum of critical flow ratios (Y)
  • proportional split
  • tuned vs untuned baseline
Do

Compute C₀ for the demo intersection from your per-approach counts. Compare it with the 60 s cycle you benchmarked against. Write down the honest sentence about the baseline.

DAY 11

Coordination, progression, and the tools engineers live in

Along a corridor, signals share a cycle length and are staggered by offsets so platoons arrive on green — the green wave, drawn on a time-space diagram, sized by its bandwidth. Two-way progression is always a compromise. When demand exceeds capacity, no timing fixes it: queues spill back, and the job becomes metering and managing gridlock. The tools: Synchro (timing plans, the industry standard), PTV Vissim (microsimulation), TRANSYT in the UK, and SUMO as the open-source microsim you now use.

Own these
  • offset · time-space diagram · bandwidth
  • progression · green wave
  • background cycle · force-off
  • oversaturation · spillback · gridlock
  • Synchro · Vissim · TRANSYT · SUMO

DAY 12

ATSPM — how agencies now manage signals

Automated Traffic Signal Performance Measures come from high-resolution (10 Hz) controller event logs. The Purdue Coordination Diagram shows when vehicles arrive relative to the green; arrivals on green is the share arriving during green; a split failure is a green that ends with queue still waiting; phase termination shows whether phases gap out, max out or get forced off. Utah DOT open-sourced the dashboard and FHWA pushed it nationally. If Theiatron emits these measures, it speaks the agency's native language.

Own these
  • hi-res data · event codes
  • Purdue Coordination Diagram (PCD)
  • arrivals on green (AoG)
  • platoon ratio
  • split failure
  • phase termination (gap-out / max-out / force-off)
  • approach delay · ped delay

DAY 13

Max-pressure and the research frontier

Max-pressure (Varaiya, 2013) serves the phase with the largest queue imbalance and is provably throughput-optimal; it is the baseline academic reviewers expect you to beat. Self-organising control (SOTL) and reinforcement learning (PressLight, CoLight, MPLight) dominate papers but not deployments, for three reasons agencies will name: safety, explainability, and the simulation-to-reality gap. Your own sweep data — that switch-eager policies lose to a fixed timer because each switch burns lost time — is a better argument than any paper.

Own these
  • max-pressure
  • throughput-optimal
  • SOTL
  • RL signal control (PressLight, CoLight)
  • sim-to-real gap
  • hysteresis
Do

Say in one breath: "Theiatron runs a max-pressure-flavoured policy with hysteresis and hard minimum-green and clearance constraints, because provable throughput is worth nothing if the controller oscillates." Then explain why you didn't "just use RL".

Read / watch

Week 3The industry: standards, safety, vendors, money

DAY 15

Standards and the hardware landscape

United States: NEMA TS2 governs cabinets and controllers electrically; the ATC family (5201 controller, 5301 cabinet, 5401 application API) defines the modern Linux controller; NTCIP 1202 is the SNMP object model central systems use to talk to signal controllers — your integration standard; MUTCD Part 4 sets the warrants that justify a signal at all. United Kingdom: TSRGD is the law, TOPAS registers approved equipment, UTMC is the open framework councils buy against, and TRL's SCOOT (networks) and MOVA (isolated junctions) are the reference adaptive products. Nigeria: FRSC federally, state ministries of transport, and in Lagos LASTMA and LAMATA.

DAY 16

Safety-critical design: the conflict monitor and flash

Every cabinet contains a hardware watchdog — the conflict monitor, or Malfunction Management Unit in TS2 — that independently checks for conflicting greens, missing reds and short yellows, and drops the junction into flash (flashing amber main street, flashing red side street) the instant anything looks wrong. Flash is the universal safe state. Preemption (rail, emergency vehicles via Opticom) and transit priority sit on top. The doctrine you must repeat: control software may request; only certified hardware enforces. Theiatron's unit decides, the safety layer guards.

Own these
  • conflict monitor · MMU
  • flash · flash transfer relay
  • conflicting greens · red failure
  • preemption vs priority (EVP, TSP)
  • fail-safe · interlock
  • certified vs custom
Do

Answer, calmly and completely: "What happens when your box crashes at 2 a.m.?" (The conflict monitor puts the junction on flash, exactly as with any controller fault; on our own integrated unit a hardware watchdog does the same.) Then explain why a software-only interlock is disqualifying.

DAY 18

What signals cost — the unit economics

Anchor numbers (US, commonly cited): a new signal installation runs roughly $250k–$500k; adding adaptive control is tens of thousands per intersection (FHWA-cited ranges around $20k–$65k) before communications and detection; loops cost thousands per zone and require pavement cuts; annual maintenance is thousands per signal; and agencies run thin, often one engineer per hundreds of signals. Value-of-time arithmetic (delay saved × vehicles × value of time, plus fuel and emissions) is how benefits get monetised in a business case.

DAY 19

How agencies buy, and how pilots actually work

Agencies buy through RFPs and framework contracts, with pilots and proofs-of-concept as the side door; funding comes from city budgets, national grants (SMART grants in the US, DfT and Innovate UK in Britain) and development banks for African cities. A credible pilot is staged — shadow mode (observe and log, no authority), then advisory, then control with hardware fail-safe — with KPIs agreed up front (control delay, arrivals-on-green, split failures), a before/after study design, clear data ownership and privacy terms (counts only, no identities), insurance, and a local maintenance plan. Traffic studies themselves are a product: Miovision-style counts are sold per intersection-day, which is your shadow-mode wedge revenue.

Objection bank

The questions you will get, with the shape of a good answer. Drill Days 23–24 from here; expand it every time a new question lands.

From engineers

Does it work at night and in the rain?

Elevated views with street lighting work today; low light and rain are known weak points of every video-detection product. Our plan: VisDrone-style fine-tuning, a thermal or radar assist where budgets allow, and honest confidence gating — when detection confidence drops, the controller falls back to a safe pretimed plan rather than guessing.

What about occlusion — a bus hiding the queue?

We count arrivals crossing an upstream line, not presence at the stop bar, so a temporarily hidden vehicle is still counted on the way in. Multi-object tracking bridges short occlusions; long ones degrade to a conservative estimate, never to a lost vehicle.

How do you handle pedestrians?

As hard constraints: minimum green includes WALK plus clearance wherever a crossing exists, and the controller can't shorten it. Pedestrian detection for call-on-demand is a roadmap item, not a launch claim.

What are your change and clearance intervals?

Set by the local standard from approach speed and crossing width — currently 3 s yellow and 1 s all-red in the demo — and fixed, not adaptive. The controller only decides how long greens last.

What happens when the unit crashes at 2 a.m.?

The same thing that happens when any controller faults: the conflict monitor or our hardware watchdog drops the junction to flash. Software only ever requests a state; hardware enforces safety.

What does it do when the junction is oversaturated?

It maximises throughput and keeps the maximum-green and starvation limits, which is all any isolated controller can do. Oversaturation is a corridor and metering problem; we say so.

What if the camera is knocked, moved or dirty?

Auto-calibration re-learns approaches from observed trajectories, and a calibration-health check detects drift and reverts to pretimed operation until it's confirmed. Lens cleaning is part of the maintenance plan like any detector.

Was your fixed-time baseline even tuned?

The demo baseline is a 30 s split. The benchmark plan compares against a Webster-optimised plan and SUMO's NEMA actuated emulation on the same replayed demand, which is the comparison we'd want a reviewer to see.

Why a camera rather than radar or loops?

Loops need pavement cuts and die at resurfacing; radar sees presence, not turning movements. One elevated camera gives counts, turning movements and queues for every approach, and yields the traffic data cities buy anyway. We'll add a sensor where the camera alone can't meet a site's requirement.

How is the simulation not rigged?

Demand is real — detected and tracked in real footage — and identical for both controllers; only the policy differs. Queue discharge is simulated because recorded vehicles can't react to our light. The next step replays the same demand through SUMO's physics and controllers we didn't write.

From investors

How big is the market?

Two markets: junctions with signals that cost tens of thousands per intersection to make adaptive, and the far larger number of junctions in fast-growing cities with no working control at all. Lead with the second and bring the numbers from Day 18.

Why now?

Edge AI hardware crossed the threshold — a Pi-class board with an AI accelerator runs detection in real time for well under a few hundred pounds — so detection and control fit in one solar-powered box with no comms dependency. That combination didn't exist five years ago.

How are you different from Vivacity or NoTraffic?

They sell sensors and cloud platforms to well-funded agencies with existing controllers. We sell an autonomous, offline, self-calibrating unit for junctions that have nothing, at an order of magnitude lower cost. Different customer, different constraint.

What's the regulatory or certification path?

Two modes: as a detector-plus-advisor peering with an existing controller over NTCIP, which needs no safety certification of our own; and as an integrated unit where the fail-safe layer is a certified conflict-monitor-style component. Shadow-mode pilots run before either.

How do you make money before control revenue?

Shadow mode produces turning-movement counts and performance data that cities already pay for per intersection-day. Data first, control second, with the same hardware.

What's defensible?

Self-calibration from trajectories, offline operation, and field data from junctions no incumbent has ever instrumented — plus the trust of the agencies that pilot first.

What are the team's gaps?

Name them: a traffic engineer adviser, an embedded hardware lead, and a public-sector sales partner in-country. Investors fund founders who know what they're missing.

How long to hardware?

Edge v0 demonstrator in weeks on off-the-shelf parts; a field-hardened shadow-mode unit in a quarter; control-capable unit with certified fail-safe in the following two to three quarters. Milestones, not vibes.
